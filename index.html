🇦🇹 Vienna: Food, Culture & What to Expect
The Cuisine

Viennese cuisine is Central European comfort food elevated to an art form — hearty, rich, and built around centuries of imperial tradition. The city sits at the crossroads of Austrian, Hungarian, Czech, and Italian influences, a legacy of the Habsburg Empire. Don't expect farm-to-table minimalism; expect butter, cream, veal, and pastry done with extraordinary care.

Must-Have Dishes
  • Wiener Schnitzel — The one non-negotiable. Made from veal (not pork), pounded thin, breaded, and pan-fried in clarified butter until golden. Served with lemon and lingonberry jam, it should arrive slightly larger than the plate. Figlmüller and Plachuttas are the canonical spots.
  • Tafelspitz — Boiled beef served in its own broth with apple-horseradish sauce, roasted potatoes, and chives — Emperor Franz Joseph's favorite meal. It sounds plain and tastes extraordinary.
  • Gulasch — In Vienna, a paprika-rich beef stew served with bread dumplings (Semmelknödel). The Gulaschmuseum serves 15 varieties.
  • Kaiserschmarrn — A shredded, caramelized pancake dessert dusted with powdered sugar and served with plum compote. Order it at any coffee house.
  • Sachertorte — Dense chocolate cake layered with apricot jam. The debate between Hotel Sacher and Demel over who makes the "original" has run since 1876. Try both.
  • The Viennese Coffee House — UNESCO-listed cultural heritage. A Melange is the standard: espresso topped with steamed milk and foam. Sit as long as you like — no one will rush you. A glass of water comes with every coffee, unprompted.
What Americans Should Know
  • Tipping: Round up or add 5–10%. Tell the server the total you want to pay — don't leave cash on the table. Twenty percent will seem excessive.
  • Dinner timing: Restaurants don't get going until 7–7:30 PM. Showing up at 5:30 PM may find you a mostly empty room.
  • Cash: Preferred at markets, smaller cafés, and traditional Gasthäuser. Keep €50–100 on hand.
  • Water: Not free and not automatically brought to the table. Tap water (Leitungswasser) is safe — you can ask for it.
  • Bread charges: Bread brought to the table is typically charged per piece. It'll appear on the bill.
  • Sundays: The Naschmarkt, many shops, and some restaurants close on Sundays. Plan Day 2 around museums and coffee houses.
  • Atmosphere: Noise levels in traditional Viennese restaurants are low and the atmosphere slightly formal. Not stuffy — just measured.
🇭🇺 Budapest: Food, Culture & What to Expect
The Cuisine

Hungarian cuisine is one of Europe's most distinctive and underrated — bold, paprika-driven, and deeply influenced by the country's nomadic Central Asian roots, Ottoman occupation, and Habsburg period. It is a cuisine of contrasts: fiery and warming, but also delicate and complex. Budapest sits at its very best.

Must-Have Dishes
  • Gulyás (Goulash) — The national dish — a paprika-rich beef and vegetable soup, not the thick stew most Americans know. Served with small pasta dumplings called csipetke.
  • Lángos — Street food: deep-fried dough topped with sour cream and grated cheese. Find it at the Great Market Hall. Greasy, satisfying, completely worth it.
  • Halászlé (Fisherman's Soup) — A spicy, intensely paprika-forward river fish soup. Scarlet red and not for the faint of heart.
  • Pörkölt — The thick paprika meat stew most Americans think of as goulash. Usually pork, beef, or chicken, served with egg noodle dumplings (nokedli).
  • Kürtőskalács (Chimney Cake) — Sweet yeast dough roasted on a spit and rolled in sugar. Get the plain cinnamon version — the Nutella-filled tourist versions are an abomination.
  • Dobos Torte — Thin sponge cake layers with chocolate buttercream and a signature caramel top. Gerbeaud Café is the place to try it.
  • Tokaji Wine — One of Europe's great sweet wines from northeastern Hungary. Aszú is the most prized version. Available everywhere and at the Great Market Hall.
  • Unicum — A dark, bitter Hungarian herbal liqueur. Hungary's answer to Fernet, served as a digestif. Deeply polarizing. Worth trying once.
What Americans Should Know
  • Currency: Hungary uses the Hungarian Forint (HUF), not the Euro. Roughly 370–390 HUF to the dollar in 2026 — things feel very inexpensive compared to Vienna.
  • ATMs & Exchange: Avoid currency exchange kiosks near the Chain Bridge and Váci Utca — misleading rates and heavy fees. Use bank-branded ATMs.
  • Tipping: 10–15% is standard and genuinely appreciated. Same convention: tell the server your total.
  • Váci Utca: The main pedestrian street near the Marriott looks beautiful but is tourist-facing — overpriced restaurants and souvenir shops. Don't eat there.
  • Thermal bath etiquette: Bring/rent a towel, bring flip-flops, bring a padlock for lockers. Swimwear required. Wednesday evenings at Széchenyi are far less crowded than weekends.
  • English: Hungarian is unrelated to any Western European language, but English is widely spoken in tourist areas and restaurants — especially by anyone under 50.
  • Ruin bars: Occupy abandoned buildings with mismatched furniture and art installations. Not just for drinking — great for atmosphere. Wednesday night is ideal.
  • Sundays: Unlike Vienna, Budapest on Sunday is lively. Restaurants, ruin bars, and most attractions are open.
